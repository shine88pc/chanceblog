Assessing how young university graduates transition into the workforce requires looking beyond headline employment rates to the quality and structure of the jobs they secure. The share of graduates holding permanent, full-time positions offers one of the clearest windows into career stability, long-term economic independence, and broader workforce health.

According to a [study released by Statistics Canada](https://www.statcan.gc.ca/daily-quotidien/261028/dq261028b-eng.htm), close to 7 in 10 young adults aged 25 to 34 years with a bachelor's degree or higher (69%) were employed in permanent, full-time positions in 2025. This figure represents a slight decrease from the 71% peak documented in 2022 during the late stages of the COVID-19 pandemic, when labour market conditions were exceptionally tight.

### Adjusting from the 2022 Peak

The 2022 high of 71% reflected a distinct macroeconomic backdrop. Widespread hiring needs and tight candidate supplies pushed employers across several sectors to offer permanent roles to attract and retain skilled entrants. Under those conditions, degree holders found relatively direct paths into full-time employment.

By 2025, that figure softened to 69%. Rather than pointing to a sudden downturn, the two-percentage-point decrease reflects an easing of post-pandemic pressures. As hiring paces normalize, access to permanent openings becomes more competitive. Still, that nearly 70% of graduates aged 25 to 34 hold permanent, full-time positions underscores that higher educational credentials continue to provide a substantial anchor in the formal workforce.

### The Wider Economic and Social Stakes

The inverse of this indicator—the roughly 31% of degree holders outside permanent, full-time employment—raises practical questions for education and economic policy. Workers outside this category typically navigate temporary contracts, part-time schedules, independent gig work, or job search periods.

Delayed entry into permanent full-time employment affects more than individual earnings. It shapes decisions around independent housing, family formation, and long-term saving. From a broader economic perspective, when educated workers spend extended periods underemployed or in transient roles, communities shoulder an indirect cost in underutilized human capital. Observing how this balance shifts in the coming years will be crucial for institutions tracking the real-world return on university education.
