Instructing emerging talent in high-pressure scientific and engineering environments is rarely a matter of finding spare time; it is a choice of fundamental priorities. When Rice University student Malvika Bhardwaj asked her mentor Daniel Cohen—a Guidance, Navigation & Control (GN&C) engineer at Johnson Space Center—how he managed to teach interns alongside a demanding workload, his response was clear: "You decide what’s important."

According to [published material](https://www.nasa.gov/general/how-a-johnson-mentor-shapes-the-next-generation-of-nasa-talent/), this exchange was highlighted in the context of recognizing mentorship contributions. In highly specialized engineering domains like GN&C—where satellite and spacecraft positioning rely on precise calculations and real-time oversight—transferring technical capability involves more than handing over standard operating procedures. It requires teaching how to evaluate risk and make critical judgments under constraints.

### Knowledge Transfer and Tacit Expertise

In technical disciplines, a significant portion of core competence exists as tacit knowledge—insights gained through practice that are difficult to articulate fully in textbooks or operation manuals. Direct guidance from practicing engineers serves as the primary mechanism for transferring this tacit knowledge to students.

- **Setting Priorities**: Choosing to dedicate time to intern instruction within a heavy technical workload frames talent development as a primary goal rather than a secondary chore.
- **Bridging Theory and Practice**: Hands-on mentorship helps university students translate theoretical concepts into actionable engineering judgment suitable for real-world missions.
- **Sustaining Technical Capabilities**: Consistent engagement with early-career researchers strengthens the workforce pipeline required for long-term aerospace projects.

### Broader Implications for STEM Workforce Development

Maintaining a resilient scientific workforce requires continuous structural support for early-career researchers. When senior technical staff active in major engineering centers systematically mentor incoming talent, organizations preserve critical skills across generational transitions.

For students, participating in direct technical dialogues provides clarity on how professional standards operate in practice. Beyond learning specific toolsets, interns observe how experienced engineers weigh competing technical demands. This process equips young researchers to handle complex systems independently as they transition into full-time roles.

Advancements in complex engineering systems ultimately depend on the individuals who design, test, and operate them. A mentor’s reminder that professionals choose what matters underscores that building future scientific capability rests on the conscious, everyday decisions of experienced engineers.
