In a [news item](https://joint-research-centre.ec.europa.eu/jrc-news-and-updates/tourism-generates-more-one-every-10-euros-value-added-eu-2026-11-04_en) dated November 4, 2026, the European Commission's Joint Research Centre (JRC) presents new estimates of tourism's economic weight. The headline claim is that more than a tenth of value added in the EU comes from tourism. The number behind that claim is more specific, and this article spells out what it counts.

The JRC illustrates the idea with a booking in Seville or a meal in Dubrovnik. The money paid at the till is only the first step: the hotel buys linen and electricity, the restaurant orders fish and wine from local suppliers, and the staff then spend their wages in nearby shops, schools and other services. Until now, the JRC says, official EU figures captured only that first step; the new estimates measure all three.

### What the 10.1% Counts

Across the EU countries included, the JRC puts tourism's total economic impact at an estimated 10.1% of gross value added (GVA), on average. The estimate includes three things: tourism's direct contribution, the knock-on effect through the businesses that supply it, and the spending of income earned within tourism. Counting only the direct part gives 4.1%, so the total is almost two and a half times the direct contribution. GVA is a gauge of the output that businesses and sectors produce, and the JRC adds that it can stand in for gross domestic product (GDP).

One calculation of ours, which assumes the total is the sum of those three parts: subtracting the rounded 4.1% from the rounded 10.1% leaves about 6 percentage points of GVA for the supply-chain and income-spending effects together. The news item does not say how those two share it.

In 2022 the direct share was 3.6%, when the sector was still recovering from the COVID-19 crisis, and it has risen since. The item does not say which year the 10.1% and 4.1% refer to.

### How Much It Varies by Country

National figures differ a great deal, the JRC notes. On the new estimate, Italy and Spain, where tourism activity is high, come out at about 14% of GVA, Portugal and Croatia go past 20%, and Belgium and Denmark fall just under 5%. The average of 10.1% therefore sits between very different national figures. The item names only these six countries and does not list the others covered.

### Why the Estimates Are New and How They Were Built

According to the JRC, the Commission is publishing estimates of its own for tourism's total economic impact for the first time. Measuring that impact is hard: the national accounts have no tourism industry as such, because tourism is defined by the type of consumer, and visitors' spending has to be separated from residents' spending, which is complicated. Tourism Satellite Accounts were designed to deal with this. At EU level, however, Eurostat's data covers only the direct effect, depends on member states choosing to submit figures and still carries the label of experimental statistics. Until now the Commission has therefore leaned on estimates from industry organisations.

The JRC's answer relies on official statistics alone. Its starting point is the Tourism Satellite Accounts that national statistical offices compile. To these it adds Eurostat's input-output tables, which record what each part of the economy buys from the others, and counts of nights spent in tourist accommodation, used to track how tourism demand evolves. With these inputs it can trace visitor spending through supply chains and into household incomes. Because every country is treated the same way, the results can be compared across Europe. A peer-reviewed paper by JRC and University of Bologna researchers, published in Current Issues in Tourism, sets out the method in full; this article did not open it.

### What the JRC Says the Estimates Are For

The JRC says its estimates feed into the EU Strategy for Sustainable Tourism, which the Commission adopted on the day of the news item, by giving the strategy a measure of the sector's economic weight. They also appear as a new indicator in the EU Tourism Dashboard. That Dashboard dates from 2022, when it was set up at the request of EU countries; the JRC builds and runs it for the Commission's mobility and transport directorate-general, DG MOVE. It gathers indicators that countries and regions can use to monitor how tourism performs environmentally, digitally and socio-economically.

The JRC's case for the work is that decision makers, from policymakers to national authorities and the organisations that manage destinations, can act on firmer ground once they see both the total impact and the differences between countries. Its examples are shaping measures of the right scale for sustainable growth, allocating public resources efficiently and checking how well tourism copes with shocks. These are the JRC's stated purposes. The news item does not report any decision taken on the basis of the estimates, and the strategy and the Dashboard were not opened for this article.
