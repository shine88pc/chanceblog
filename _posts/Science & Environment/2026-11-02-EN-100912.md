NASA's November Night Sky Notes column, dated October 30, 2026, reports that [Voyager 1 will reach a distance of one light-day](https://science.nasa.gov/solar-system/skywatching/night-sky-network/novembers-night-sky-notes-light-day-away/) from the Sun on November 18, 2026. The column puts that distance at about 16.1 billion miles and notes that the milestone arrives 49 years after the probe's launch in 1977. A light-day is the distance light covers in one day.

Despite the series name, the column does not offer November observing targets or stargazing tips. It is about Voyager and about measuring distance in units of light, and it points out that a light-minute measures distance, not time.

### The 1977 launch and the alignment behind it

Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 are twin probes launched in 1977. The column says they took advantage of an arrangement of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune that comes around once every 175 years. The arrangement allowed a total of six gravity assists between the two probes on a multi-planet journey the column calls the Grand Tour. A gravity assist is a flyby in which a planet's gravity changes a spacecraft's speed and direction.

At Saturn the two probes parted ways, and Voyager 2 went on to Uranus and Neptune. The column adds that both spacecraft still send back science data and that each carries a 12-inch Golden Record with greetings, music and images from Earth.

### What the column offers readers

The column also points to things readers can try or look up, such as an activity that shows how a gravity assist works, an activity guide for measuring a light-minute, and a NASA page that tracks where the two Voyagers are now.
