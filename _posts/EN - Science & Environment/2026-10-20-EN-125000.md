Seeing a G3 or G4 alert in a space-weather app does not mean that a blackout or communications failure is about to happen where you live. NOAA’s G scale describes possible effects of disturbances in Earth’s magnetic field on systems such as power grids, satellites, and navigation. To understand what an alert means, read its **event type, whether it is a forecast or an observation, and which systems and regions could be affected**. The number alone is not a local impact forecast.

## G, R, and S describe different events

The NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center (SWPC) separates impacts into three scales. In [NOAA’s explanation](https://www.spaceweather.gov/noaa-scales-explanation), G refers to geomagnetic storms, R to radio blackouts associated with solar flares, and S to solar radiation storms caused by energetic particles. A headline that says only “solar storm” may blur these distinct phenomena, so checking the letter is a useful first step.

The G scale runs from G1 (minor) through G5 (extreme). NOAA relates it to the planetary Kp index, which summarizes changes in Earth’s magnetic field using observations from magnetometers around the world. Kp is reported on a 0–9 scale and calculated for three-hour intervals. Roughly, Kp 5 corresponds to G1, 6 to G2, 7 to G3, 8 to G4, and 9 to G5. Kp measures the disturbance; it does not calculate the chance of a power outage in a particular town. NOAA identifies power grids, spacecraft operations, radio signals that pass through the ionosphere, and aurora observers among the groups that may be affected. See the [NOAA Kp-index explanation](https://soho.nascom.nasa.gov/data/synoptic/sel/planetary-k-index).

| Scale | What it describes | Possible effects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| G1–G5 | Geomagnetic storms | Grid-voltage fluctuations, satellite operations and navigation errors, changes in aurora visibility |
| R1–R5 | Solar-flare radio blackouts | Weaker high-frequency (HF) radio communications on the sunlit side of Earth |
| S1–S5 | Solar energetic particles | Radiation concerns for astronauts and high-altitude polar flights; effects on spacecraft equipment |

## A scale lists possibilities, not guaranteed outcomes

At G1, NOAA lists possible weak grid fluctuations and minor effects on satellite operations. At G3, possible effects include voltage corrections, false alarms in some protection systems, intermittent satellite-navigation problems, and increased drag on low-Earth-orbit satellites. At G5, the scale describes the possibility of widespread voltage-control problems and satellite navigation degraded for days. These are potential effects, not a promise that every listed problem will occur in every location. Duration, magnetic conditions, infrastructure, and satellite position all shape the outcome.

GPS needs a little context. A possible degradation in satellite navigation does not mean every phone will lose its map service. The concern can be more relevant to operations that rely on precise positioning or timing, including surveying, aviation, maritime navigation, or grid synchronization. For an ordinary user, the scale by itself is not evidence of a local service disruption; check local authorities or a service provider for an actual notice.

There is also a difference between direct radiation risk to people on the ground and effects on technology. [NASA explains that Earth’s atmosphere and magnetic field shield people on the ground from the direct harmful effects of solar storms](https://science.nasa.gov/sun/solar-storms-and-flares/). Astronauts and crews on high-altitude polar flights face different exposure conditions and are managed separately.

## Four things to check in an alert

1. **The letter:** Is it a G geomagnetic storm, an R radio blackout, or an S radiation storm?
2. **The status:** Is the page showing a forecast or an observed maximum? NOAA presents these separately.
3. **The affected system and region:** Check whether the notice concerns grids, satellites, HF radio, or polar aviation. Aurora visibility also depends on geomagnetic latitude, clouds, and light pollution; a G rating alone cannot promise a sighting.
4. **The update time:** Conditions can change as new observations arrive. Recheck the [live NOAA scales page](https://www.spaceweather.gov/noaa-scales-explanation) instead of relying on an old screenshot.

Space-weather alerts help infrastructure operators prepare; they are not a countdown to a household emergency. For readers, the useful questions are concrete: which phenomenon is being reported, what could it affect, and is the information observed or forecast?
