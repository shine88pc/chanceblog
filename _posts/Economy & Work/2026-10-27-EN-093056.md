Demand for public co-financing across Europe’s energy interconnectors has reached a substantial scale. According to an announcement from the [European Climate, Infrastructure and Environment Executive Agency (CINEA)](https://cinea.ec.europa.eu/news-events/news/cef-energy-2026-call-pcipmi-projects-attracts-strong-interest-67-applications-requesting-eur288-2026-10-26_en), the European Commission has received 67 applications requesting €2.88 billion in EU funding under the Connecting Europe Facility (CEF) Energy 2026 call.

The call covers Projects of Common Interest (PCIs) and Projects of Mutual Interest (PMIs)—cross-border infrastructure developments aimed at interconnecting EU member states and partner countries. While these submissions represent proposals rather than final grants, the total requested sum provides a concrete metric of where European utilities and project sponsors are seeking to deploy capital.

### The Industrial Logic Behind the Influx of Applications

Europe’s aggressive expansion of renewable capacity has exposed critical physical constraints in existing transmission networks. Wind farms in coastal waters and solar installations in southern regions frequently face grid curtailment or transmission bottlenecks when sending electricity toward heavy industrial demand centers. Mitigating this volatility requires high-voltage interconnectors, expanded storage facilities, and resilient transport corridors capable of moving energy across borders.

However, cross-border infrastructure assets carry high upfront capital expenditures, lengthy regulatory cycles, and prolonged amortization periods. For commercial lenders and national transmission system operators (TSOs), direct EU co-funding serves as a crucial risk-mitigation tool. The 67 submissions indicate that project promoters view public co-investment as an essential catalyst for projects that might otherwise struggle with private capital costs or cross-jurisdictional financial hurdles.

### Economic Stakes for Industry, Labor, and Consumers

Energy infrastructure allocations translate into concrete economic consequences across supply chains and household budgets:

- **Specialized Engineering and Manufacturing Demand**: Projects of this scale drive procurement orders for high-voltage direct current (HVDC) cables, converter stations, substations, and civil engineering services, creating sustained demand for technical labor.
- **Wholesale Power Price Equalization**: Removing cross-border transmission bottlenecks allows cheaper, surplus renewable power to flow to high-cost markets, dampening regional wholesale price volatility for commercial users.
- **Mitigating Tariff Hikes for Consumers**: Without public co-funding mechanisms, the full cost of grid modernization is typically recovered through regulated transmission network tariffs added to household and industrial retail utility bills. Public capital absorption helps mitigate rapid escalation in these consumer-facing fees.

### What the Numbers Represent—and What Remains Pending

It is important to emphasize that the €2.88 billion figure reflects requested funding, not allocated capital. CINEA and the European Commission will now evaluate the 67 applications against criteria including cross-border security of supply, climate impact, project maturity, and commercial viability.

Only a selected group of proposals that meet the evaluation threshold within the call's available budget will proceed to grant agreement negotiations. The application volume illustrates the current investment appetite in European energy networks, but actual economic deployment will depend on the final selection decisions and subsequent construction schedules.
